<section id="overview">
<span class="eyebrow">01 — Project overview</span>
<h2>Climbing multiple bars, carrying the whole robot</h2>

I designed a custom deployable climbing mechanism for our 2026 FIRST Robotics Competition robot to
complete the multi-bar endgame climb. The system uses alternating static and moving hooks to
sequentially ascend multiple horizontal bars while staying inside strict FRC size, weight, and
timing constraints.

I was responsible for the **mechanical design, CAD, and the engineering decisions** behind the
mechanism.

<div class="split">
  <div class="txt">
    <h3>The constraint that shaped everything</h3>
    <p>The climber had to start completely inside the robot frame perimeter, reach a first bar
    27 in. above the floor without exceeding the 30 in. extension limit, and fit into the gaps
    between the swerve drives, drivebase, and shooter — about three inches of width on each
    side.</p>
    <p>Everything else — the pivot architecture, the transmission split, the hook geometry —
    follows from packaging a full-robot-weight load path into that space.</p>

<section id="architecture">
<span class="eyebrow">03 — Major engineering decisions</span>
<h2>Pivot, transmission, and load path</h2>

### Dead-axle pivot

The climber pivot experiences some of the highest loads on the robot while operating in a very
constrained packaging envelope. I selected a **dead-axle pivot** because it provides a rigid load
path while simplifying the bearing arrangement. That architecture also let the drivetrain be split
into two power-reduction stages, reducing the amount of reduction required in the motor gearbox.

### Power transmission

<div class="split rev">
  <Fig src={img('climber/cad-top-transparent.png')} alt="Transparent top-down CAD view showing the belt reduction, intermediate shaft, and gear reduction into the climber pivot"
       label="CAD" caption="Transparent top view: belt reduction from the motor to the intermediate shaft, then a gear reduction into the climber pivot." />
  <div class="txt">
    <p>The pivot is driven by a <b>belt reduction</b> between the motor and an intermediate shaft,
    followed by a <b>gear reduction</b> from that shaft to the climber pivot.</p>
    <p>The belt stage was selected because the expected loading did not justify a chain drive, and
    belts allowed predictable center-to-center spacing with minimal tensioning requirements. The
    gear stage provided a compact way to transfer torque within the limited packaging space
    available around the pivot.</p>
    <p>The climbing motion itself is driven by <b>chain</b> — it has to support the full weight of
    the robot during ascent.</p>
  </div>
</div>

### Hooks

The mechanism uses alternating static and moving **carbon fiber hooks** to climb multiple bars
sequentially. Carbon fiber was selected to minimize weight while maintaining enough strength to
support the robot. I performed minor **finite element analysis (FEA)** during the design process to
evaluate the structural performance of the hook geometry.

### Structural design

Linear rails and elevator bearing blocks were selected because they are rated to withstand the
large horizontal loads generated when the climber pivots under the robot's weight. Heavy
components, including motors, were intentionally packaged as low as possible in the robot to
maintain a low center of gravity and improve climbing stability.
</section>

<section id="lessons">
<span class="eyebrow">06 — Lessons learned</span>
<h2>What I would do differently</h2>

This project reinforced the importance of balancing mechanical performance, packaging constraints,
manufacturability, and overall robot weight.

If I were to redesign the mechanism, I would:

- Focus on **reducing mass earlier** in the design process
- **Simplify the hook architecture**
- Validate critical climbing interactions through **earlier prototype testing**
